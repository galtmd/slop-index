The Slop Index

A growing share of what is published as medical and public health research is politics in the costume of science. The conclusion is settled before the study begins, the data are gathered because a journal will not print an opinion without a table, and the abstract exists to lend an argument the authority of a citation. The result is a literature that confirms, at length and with confidence intervals, propositions that were never in doubt, and that treats the harder questions of mechanism, intervention, and outcome as somebody else's business. None of this shows up in the h-index, which counts a commentary cited by other commentaries exactly as it counts a trial that changed practice.

The Slop Index is my attempt to characterize that difference. It does not ask whether a topic is political; medicine has always been political, and some of the most important work in epidemiology concerns poverty, race, and policy. It asks whether a paper on such a topic earned its place the way any other paper must, with a real design, a hard endpoint, and an effect the field found worth citing, or whether the subject matter was doing the work that the science should have done. The aim is to distinguish actual contributions to knowledge about contested questions from advocacy that has borrowed the vocabulary of research, and to do it with rules anyone can read, run, and argue with.

What is the h-index counting?

The h-index counts citations without asking what was cited. A viewpoint in JAMA and a randomized trial in JAMA count the same. This tool scores every PubMed-indexed paper by an author on three axes, reports each separately, and combines them into one labeled composite, the Total Slop Index. Everything runs in your browser: PubMed and NIH iCite are queried directly, and the scoring code is the open-source Python package executed in-page.

Reading the number

00.470.570.651
researchmixedcommentary

Zero is good and one is bad, and the useful cutoff is 0.57. That number is not arbitrary: it is the score of a bibliography whose average paper has nothing to recommend it and nothing to penalize, so anything above it means the typical paper is net negative under the weights, and anything below it means the typical paper adds something. The calibration table turns that into three practical bands. Below 0.47 is a research record: bench scientists, trialists, and surgeons and statisticians with hard endpoints land here, from about 0.18 (Karikó) to 0.44. 0.47 to 0.65 is mixed: real empirical work with enough penalized topics or commentary to pull the average toward zero; health economists and the senator-elect epidemiologist sit here. Above 0.65 is a commentary record: the median paper is negative, most of the output is penalized topics, a third or more is opinion. Nobody on the calibration table scored above 0.81 or below 0.18, so a result outside that range deserves a second look at the author match before it is believed.

Two cautions on any single number. A short record (under about 15 papers) swings wildly on one or two papers, so quote the band rather than the decimal. And always quote the topic-neutral score beside it; the gap between the two is the size of the editorial position, and the reader is entitled to know it.

Read before quoting a number

A score is only as good as the author match and the index behind it. PubMed does not index books, op-eds, policy reports, or most economics and social-science journals, and its full-name indexing begins around 2002, so early careers are clipped. The classifier is rule-based and conservative; a paper it cannot place lands in commentary with a low-confidence flag. The interpretation page shows where seventeen named researchers fall, so you can see what 0.3 or 0.7 means before you quote one.
